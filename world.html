Two technologies, two kinds of work, two geographies.

AI reaches cognitive work, which is concentrated in rich countries. Robotics reaches routine manual work, which is concentrated in poorer ones. The two answers to “who loses” are not rival estimates of one number — they are different questions, and the third layer asks which countries have a reason to act on either.

    One technology at a time

    Version 4 above turns the map over to a single technology. Search by name, by developer, by the developer's country or by an occupation code; filter by whether it targets cognitive or manual work, and by how far along it is. The developer's country is outlined on the map, so invention and displacement appear in the same frame — and the scarcity weighting reverses the reading, from where the work is done to where the machine plausibly pencils out.

    Building this outWebb's patent-task overlap gives occupation-level scores for three broad categories — AI, software and robotics — not per-device figures, so a specific machine needs its own patent selection run through the same verb-noun matching, or a hand-coded list of target occupations. The binding constraint is employment data rather than Webb: ILOSTAT reports ISCO at 2-digit for most countries and 3- or 4-digit for few, so occupation-specific technologies will resolve for a limited set. Worth choosing the first entries partly on which occupations are reported finely enough to shade.
