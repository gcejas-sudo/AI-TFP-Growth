AI and Total Factor Productivity Interactive

Same formula. Ten times apart.

Acemoglu (2024) and Aghion & Bunel (2024) run an identical task-based equation for what AI does to US total factor productivity over a decade. Three inputs differ substantially, the fourth barely at all, and multiplication turns that into a tenfold gap. Turn the dials yourself.

GDP share of exposed tasks

How much of the economy's work does AI touch at all?

20.0%
How each side gets there

Acemoglu

20%

Tasks exposed to AI and computer vision, from Eloundou et al. (2024), combined into occupations using BLS wage bills. The wage-bill-weighted share of exposed occupations is read as a GDP share.

Aghion & Bunel

60%

Employment exposed to AI in the US, from Pizzinelli et al. (2023), who measure exposure by abilities. Abilities are weighted by prevalence and importance and aggregated to an AIOE score per occupation, then to employment shares by country. US-specific, and includes both high- and low-complementarity employment.

Key differenceAcemoglu uses task exposure, Aghion ability exposure. Aghion flags every occupation above the AIOE median as exposed; Acemoglu uses each occupation's share of exposed tasks and weights by wage bill to reach a GDP share. Aghion applies no weighting, assuming the GDP share of exposed tasks is similar in magnitude to the task share — despite working from abilities.

Share profitable to automate

Of the computer-vision tasks AI touches, how many are feasible and profitable to hand over within ten years?

23.0%
How each side gets there

Acemoglu

23%

Svanberg et al. (2024), baseline scenario.

Aghion & Bunel

50%

Svanberg et al. (2024) cost-decline scenarios, under a 22% annual decline in computing costs from Besiroglu & Hobbhahn (2022).

Key differenceAghion incorporates projected computing-cost declines. Acemoglu excludes them from his baseline, arguing that a doubling of GPU capacity will not necessarily translate into a 50% fall in costs, citing diminishing returns, bottlenecks from other inputs, and current architectural limits.

Average cost savings

Where a task is automated, how much cheaper does the labour get?

27.0%
How each side gets there

Acemoglu

27%

Average of two experimental studies: Noy & Zhang (2023) and Brynjolfsson et al. (2023).

Aghion & Bunel

40%

Average of three: Noy & Zhang (2023), Brynjolfsson et al. (2023) and Peng et al. (2023).

Key differenceAghion includes Peng et al. Acemoglu excludes it because he believes its setting — computer programmers using GitHub Copilot — is less likely to be relevant to other tasks and occupations.

AI-exposure-adjusted labour share

Tasks run on labour and capital together, so only the labour slice of the cost can be saved.

53.5%
How each side gets there

Acemoglu

53.5%

Industry-level AI exposure from Eloundou et al. (2024) combined with industry labour shares from the BEA.

Aghion & Bunel

57%

Taken straight from Acemoglu's April 2024 draft.

Key differenceNone in methodology — the same construction, one draft apart. This is the input the two sides effectively agree on, and the only place where the gap is rounding rather than argument.

Notes

Reading the gap

None of the three real disagreements is extreme on its own: 3×, 2.2× and 1.5×. Multiplied, they are roughly tenfold, and the labour-share rounding adds a further 7%. A formula this short leaves nowhere for the differences to be absorbed.

What the dials cannot show

Both figures are cumulative gains within a decade, spread evenly to reach an annual rate. Neither side is modelling adoption timing, new tasks AI might create, or reallocation across industries — the formula prices only cost savings in tasks that already exist.
